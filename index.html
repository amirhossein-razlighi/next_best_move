The idea

The model sees a board and draws its move

We fine-tune a pretrained visual diffusion model on board images and their next best moves. It marks a move by tinting the origin square blue and the destination square red. The move is applied, the opponent replies, and the new board is fed back in. There is no search, no engine and no rule module at inference: each move comes from a single generation.

Abstract

In this paper, we show that visual diffusion models can learn to predict the next best move (NBM) in tasks requiring sequential reasoning and lookahead, without explicitly exploring future steps or exhaustive searching at inference. Previous work demonstrated that these models can generate complete solutions to geometric and reasoning problems. Here, we show that simple fine-tuning on visual state–NBM pairs enables a pretrained model to select actions whose value depends on consequences several steps ahead. Each move is predicted directly in image space from the current state, without an explicit symbolic representation or search procedure. Repeated NBM predictions then construct a solution one decision at a time.

We demonstrate this capability on chess puzzles and tile-based pathfinding, and show that it extends to Connect Four, Go and billiards. In chess, the model predicts legal moves and finds the best move far more often than by recalling similar training positions, including piece sacrifices whose benefit emerges only through later tactical continuations. In pathfinding, it constructs shortest paths on uniform-cost maps and cost-optimal routes across terrain tiles with different traversal costs, choosing longer routes when they are cheaper overall. These costs are learned implicitly from examples, without numerical cost supervision. These results show that direct visual move prediction can support globally informed decisions and successful sequential problem solving.

Method

State is an image, action is an edit, decisions are samples

No new modules and no architecture change: we adapt Qwen-Image (one move per call) and the video model Wan2.2-TI2V-5B (several moves per clip) with LoRA, keeping their rectified-flow training objective.

01

Actions as image edits

The target is the input with the move drawn on it, M(x, a): every other pixel is unchanged. A rule-free decoder D fits the tint of each square and returns the strongest blue and strongest red square. A move that cannot be played counts as a failure.

D reads d4 → f6: Qxf6#. The only text input is a fixed prompt: “White to move. Highlight the best move: the origin square in blue and the destination square in red.”

02

Closed loop

The environment applies the decoded move (and, in games, the opponent's reply), renders the new state, and the model is called again. Below: the image model's actual inputs and outputs on a held-out terrain map; the red disc marks the next cell.

03

Horizon: Decide and Imagine

The video model draws several moves in one clip, alternating Decide frames (the move marked, plus green dots on the moving piece's legal squares) and Imagine frames (the board after it), replies included. Against an opponent only the first move is played.

Drawing the future helps decide the present. A video model trained to draw the whole solution line, replies included, predicts the next move more accurately than the same model trained on the first move alone, although only the first move is scored (24.6% against 21.4% in chess, same 400,000 puzzles and budget). Against an opponent, a new prediction on the observed board is better than following the drawn continuation (52.5% against 41.2% for the next move on identical boards).
Cost-aware pathfinding

Longer, but cheaper: costs it was never told

Entering floor, mud and sand costs 1, 2 and 4, but these numbers are never drawn or stated; the model only sees example routes. Calling the image model once per move on held-out maps, it walks around expensive bands when that is cheaper, and steps into them when going around costs more.

The surprising part

One step reads the decision; full sampling draws one

At every denoising step the model estimates the clean output. Its first estimate shows several candidate moves as faint, overlapping marks, and the strongest one is usually right. Full sampling instead follows each noise draw to a single move. Scrub through the 20 steps of four independent samples of one held-out puzzle (Table 1 image model).

step 1 / 20

Each panel is the model's unaltered estimate of the clean image at that step (noise level σ from 1 to 0.02). Bars: strength of each candidate's marks (1 = a fully drawn mark).

Why: the first estimate is an average

Training gives the model a distribution π(m | c) over the moves m of a board c. The best prediction of the clean output from a noisy one is the mean of all clean outputs that could have produced it. At full noise, where the input says nothing about the move, that mean is the average of the move images Im, weighted by their probabilities (Tweedie's formula):

x^0=E[x0∣c]=∑mπ(m∣c)Im

So a single step returns the whole distribution at once, and its strongest marks pick out the most likely move. Many steps turn that average into one draw, chosen in proportion to its probability: right less often, but more diverse.

Because the average hardly depends on the noise, repeated one-step outputs agree: eight of them contain the answer for only 69.2% of puzzles, against 85.4% for eight full samples. One step is the better answer; sampling is the better source of candidates. Routes, by contrast, need full sampling: the average of several routes is not itself a route.

Recall or play?

One added bishop changes the best move, and the model switches

01 drop in the model's confidence in its move when that piece is removed

On a held-out puzzle (rated 1932) the model plays the rook sacrifice Rxh6+, which forces mate in three. Adding a white bishop on c3 leaves the board almost unchanged visually, yet the bishop now guards h8, so Qh8# mates at once, and the model switches to exactly this move.

Removing one piece at a time shows what each decision rests on: the sacrifice depends on the pieces that build the later mating net; with the added bishop, the decision depends most on the bishop guarding h8 and Black's own pawn on g6, which blocks the king's only escape.

Over thousands of such changes and exact symmetries, the models' choices track the position while retrieval does not:

Beyond chess

The same recipe, unchanged

Whatever a player observes fits in one image, and the action becomes an edit of it: a disc in a column, a stone on a point, a cue direction and power. Gravity, the rules of Go and the physics of the table are never stated; the prompt is simply “Continue.” (or the side to move).
